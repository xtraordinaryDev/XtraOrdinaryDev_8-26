How Long Does Custom Software Take to Build?

By Xavier Thurman · September 14, 2026 · 7 min read

Here is the honest answer: a tightly scoped first release — one user type, one workflow, no complicated integrations — is generally discussed in the software world in terms of weeks. A real platform with multiple roles, payments, reporting, and a connection to systems you already run is generally discussed in months. Anything advertised as "a few days" is either a template with your logo on it or a demo that will not survive contact with your actual business. The single biggest variable is not the code. It is how clearly the scope is defined and how fast decisions get made on your side.

First, define what "done" means

Most timeline arguments are really scope arguments wearing a disguise. A business owner asks how long the software will take, picturing the finished thing they imagined in the shower. The developer answers based on a much smaller slice. Both walk away with different calendars in their heads.

So before you estimate anything, decide what your first release is. A first release is the smallest version of the system that a real person can use to do real work. Not a prototype. Not a demo. Something that replaces a spreadsheet, an inbox, or a clipboard on day one.

For a contractor, that might be one screen where the office staff logs jobs and a second screen where crews see today's assignments. Invoicing, photo uploads, and customer notifications come later. For a clinic, it might be intake forms that land in a searchable queue — not the whole patient management universe.

Every feature you push out of release one pulls the launch date closer. Every feature you pull in pushes it out. That trade is the entire negotiation.

The phases, and where the time actually goes

Discovery and written scope

This is where somebody sits with you and writes down exactly what the software will do, screen by screen, rule by rule. What happens when a user submits a form twice. What an admin can see that a customer cannot. What the system does when a payment fails.

Owners often want to skip this phase because it does not look like progress. It is the phase that saves the most time. We quote every project as a fixed price with a written scope before work starts, which means the ambiguity gets resolved on paper instead of halfway through the build when changing it is expensive.

Data model and architecture

Before screens get built, someone has to decide how your information is structured. What is a "job"? Can a job have more than one customer? Can a customer exist without an address? These sound like small questions. Get them wrong and you pay for it for years, because every report and every feature after that has to work around the mistake.

This phase is short in calendar time and disproportionately important. It is also where enterprise experience shows up. Building internal applications at places like U.S. Bank and CHS Inc. teaches you which shortcuts come back to bite.

Build

The part everyone pictures. Screens, logic, permissions, the database, the integrations. This is usually the longest single phase, and it scales roughly with the number of distinct screens and the number of rules behind them, not with how pretty it looks.

Good builds are reviewable in pieces. You should be clicking through working software while it is being built, not waiting for a grand reveal at the end.

Testing, data migration, and launch

The phase that gets underestimated most often. Testing is not just "does the button work." It is what happens with bad data, half-finished entries, two people editing the same record, and a user on a phone with one bar of service.

Then there is migration. If you have five years of records in spreadsheets or an old system, moving them in cleanly is its own small project. Messy legacy data is one of the most common reasons a launch slips.

What makes a project take longer

  • Integrations with systems you do not control. A payment processor, a scheduling tool, an accounting system, an old database. Each one adds unknowns, and some vendors have slow approval processes or thin documentation.
  • More than two or three user roles. Admin, staff, customer, vendor, manager — each role multiplies the permission rules and the testing.
  • Compliance requirements. Health data, financial data, and anything touching sensitive records brings extra care around access, logging, and storage.
  • Undecided decisions. If we need to know your approval workflow and nobody can tell us for two weeks, that is two weeks.
  • Scope added mid-build. Not forbidden, just not free. Every addition trades against the date.
  • Content and assets. Software waits on logos, legal copy, price lists, and email templates more often than people expect.

What makes a project go faster

  • One decision-maker. A single person who can say yes without convening a committee is worth more than any tool or framework.
  • A ruthless first release. Cut features until it hurts a little, then ship and add.
  • Clean sample data. Give the team real records early — ten actual jobs, ten actual invoices — not made-up placeholders.
  • Fast feedback. Reviewing a build within a day or two, not a week, compounds across the whole project.
  • Accepting boring solutions. A well-built form beats a clever custom interface that takes three times as long and confuses your staff.

Three project shapes and how they compare

An internal tool

One team, one workflow, replacing a spreadsheet. Job tracking, inventory, intake queue, approval routing. This is the fastest category because you control the users, you can train them directly, and the design does not need to impress strangers.

A customer-facing portal

Now outsiders log in. That means accounts, password resets, permissions, clear error messages, mobile behavior, and design that reflects your brand. Meaningfully longer than an internal tool of the same size, because the margin for confusion is smaller. Portals also tend to involve payments or documents, which brings integrations with them.

A platform or AI system

GridLink, a full-stack procurement platform for enterprise fuel buying, and FundStart AI, a custom AI/RAG research platform for the AEC industry, are both in this category. Multiple user types, real business rules, and in the AI case a retrieval layer that has to be tuned against actual documents to be trustworthy. These are measured in months, and they are built in stages on purpose — something usable early, then expanded.

Software is not a website timeline

Worth saying plainly, because the two get confused. A marketing website is mostly content, design, and structure. Custom software has state, permissions, and data that changes over time — which is why it takes longer and why the testing phase is real.

If what you actually need is a fast, well-built site that generates leads, that is a different and shorter conversation. We do both, and we will tell you which one your problem calls for. Sometimes the answer is that a simple custom website plus a form and a spreadsheet handles it fine, and you do not need software yet.

How to plan around the date

Pick a business event, not a calendar date. A season start, a contract renewal, a busy quarter. Then work backward and ask what the minimum useful version looks like by then. That reframes the whole project from "build everything" to "be useful by the date that matters."

And build in room after launch. The two or three weeks following a release — when real users find the edges — are part of the project, not an afterthought. Plan for adjustments instead of being surprised by them.

If you have a process you are trying to get out of spreadsheets, the most useful next step is a conversation about scope, not a guess at a number. That is where custom software development in Minneapolis starts for us: figure out the smallest version that helps, write it down, price it, then build it. You own the code either way.
