Here is the short answer: a designer decides how it looks and how it feels to use. A developer makes it actually work in a browser. Most business projects need both skills, but almost never in equal measure. The useful question is not "which job title do I hire?" It is "what is the hard part of my project?" Answer that and the right hire becomes obvious.

The two roles in plain English

A web designer works on appearance and experience. Layout, type, color, hierarchy, what a visitor sees first, where the buttons go, what the site communicates about you in the first three seconds. The output is usually a set of screens: a home page, an interior page, a mobile view, maybe a small style guide.

A web developer turns that into a working thing. Real HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. Forms that send. Pages that load fast on a phone with two bars. A content system you can update. Databases, logins, payments, connections to the other software you already run.

A design that has never been built is a picture. Code with no design behind it is a page that works and looks like it was made in 2011. You need both. What you have to figure out is which one your project leans on.

The quick test: what is the hard part?

Write down, in one sentence, the thing that would make this project a failure if you got it wrong. Then see which list below it lands in.

Signs the hard part is design

  • Your site works fine, but it looks dated and you are losing credibility on first impression.
  • You have no logo, no colors, no consistent look across your truck wraps, proposals, and website.
  • People land on your home page and cannot tell what you do or who you serve.
  • You are a restaurant, gallery, event, or anything where the look is the product.
  • The pages are all there, but nobody clicks the thing you want them to click.

If most of those describe you, your money should go toward design first. A great developer building a bad layout faster is not progress.

Signs the hard part is development

  • You need customers to log in and see something specific to them.
  • Something in your business runs on a spreadsheet that three people edit and nobody trusts.
  • You want scheduling, quoting, payments, inventory, or documents handled on the site instead of over email.
  • Your site takes forever to load, breaks on phones, or goes down when traffic spikes.
  • You already have a site or an application and you need someone to take it over, fix it, and keep it alive.
  • Two systems you pay for do not talk to each other and someone re-types data between them.

That is engineering work. It is invisible to visitors when it is done well and extremely visible when it is not. This is the territory of custom software development in Minneapolis more than of a design engagement.

Signs you need both, in that order

Most brochure-style business sites land here. A contractor, clinic, law firm, or nonprofit usually needs a clear look and a fast, hand-built site to put it on. In that case you are not choosing between two people. You are choosing whether to hire two vendors or one team that does both. More on that in a minute.

The roles nobody warns you about

"Designer" and "developer" are the two words you hear, but a website project quietly involves a few more jobs. Someone has to do them, and if you do not assign them, they land on you at the worst possible moment.

  • Copywriting. The words on the page. Designers will fill the layout with placeholder text and expect you to replace it. This is the single most common reason projects stall.
  • Photography. Real photos of your crew, your space, your work. Stock photos of a smiling person in a hard hat fool nobody.
  • SEO and structure. Page titles, URLs, headings, service pages that match how people actually search. Best handled while the site is being built, not bolted on after.
  • Hosting and maintenance. Where the site lives, who renews the domain, who patches things.

Ask any prospective hire which of these they cover and which they do not. A straight answer here tells you more about someone than their portfolio does.

Where projects go wrong

The handoff gap

You hire a designer, get beautiful screens, then hand them to a developer who says half of it will not work responsively and the animations will hurt load time. Now you are mediating between two vendors who each think the other one is the problem. If you go the two-vendor route, get them talking in week one, not at delivery.

The designer who "also builds sites"

Plenty of designers assemble sites in a page builder. Sometimes that is genuinely enough, and we will say so. But understand what you are getting: a stack of plugins, a monthly subscription, and a ceiling on what can be added later. When you eventually need a customer portal or an integration, that work usually starts over. We wrote about that trade-off in more detail in our post comparing WordPress to a custom-coded site.

The developer with no eye

The mirror image. Everything functions, nothing persuades. If a developer's portfolio all looks like an admin dashboard and you are selling to consumers, keep looking or pair them with a designer.

Buying a redesign when you needed a rebuild

Sometimes the complaint is "it looks old," but the real problem is a slow, fragile site underneath. New paint on a bad foundation feels good for about a month. A proper website redesign should address the code and the search visibility, not just the colors.

Questions to ask before you hire either one

  1. Who owns the code, content, and domain when we are done? The answer should be you, without conditions.
  2. Is this a fixed price with a written scope, or hourly? Both can be fine. You just need to know which, in writing, before anything starts.
  3. What is not included? Copy, photos, hosting, ongoing changes. Get the exclusions listed.
  4. Can I update it myself, and what exactly can I change? "Yes" is not an answer. "You can edit text, images, and blog posts; layout changes come to us" is.
  5. What happens in a year when I want a new feature? Listen for whether the answer involves rebuilding.
  6. Show me something you built that is still live and being used. Not a concept. A working site with a real business behind it.

The one-sentence version

If your problem is how it looks, start with a designer. If your problem is what it does, start with a developer. If it is both, hire one team that owns the whole outcome so there is nobody to point at but them.
