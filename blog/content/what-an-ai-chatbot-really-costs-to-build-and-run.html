An AI chatbot has two budgets, and most people only ask about one. There is the build — scoping, content prep, integrations, testing — which is a one-time cost that depends almost entirely on how much the bot is allowed to do. Then there is the run — model usage, hosting, monitoring, and someone reviewing what it says — which is small but never stops. A simple bot that answers questions from your existing pages is an inexpensive, contained project. A bot that looks up a customer's account, books an appointment, or quotes a price is a software project that happens to have a chat window on the front.

Below is what actually sits inside each number, so you can read a proposal and know what you are paying for.

The build: what you are actually paying someone to do

Nobody charges you for "the AI." The model is a commodity you rent by the word. What you pay a developer for is everything around it.

Scoping and guardrails

The first real work is deciding what the bot must never do. Should it quote prices? Promise a timeline? Give medical or legal guidance? Say "we can do that" about work you do not do? Every one of those is a written rule, and rules have to be tested, not just typed into a prompt. This is the step small projects skip and then regret.

Content preparation

A chatbot is only as good as what it reads. If your answers live in clean, current web pages, this step is short. If they live in a 60-page PDF from 2019, three Google Docs, and the owner's head, someone has to collect, clean, and structure all of that first. We have seen content prep quietly become the biggest line item on a chatbot build, and it is almost always worth doing because the same cleanup improves your website too.

Retrieval and indexing

If the bot needs to answer from your documents, it needs a retrieval layer — the thing that finds the three relevant paragraphs and hands them to the model before it answers. This is the RAG pattern. It is well understood engineering, but it is engineering: chunking documents sensibly, choosing how search works, and handling the case where nothing relevant is found so the bot says "I don't know" instead of inventing something. We built FundStart AI, a custom AI research platform for the architecture and construction industry, on exactly this pattern, and the retrieval quality mattered far more than the model choice.

Integrations

This is the fork in the road. A bot that only talks is cheap. A bot that does things — pulls an order status, checks availability, creates a ticket, writes to your CRM — needs authenticated access to those systems, error handling for when they are down, and a clear log of every action taken on a customer's behalf. If your scheduling or billing system has a clean, documented API, this is manageable. If it does not, that one integration can cost more than the rest of the bot combined.

Testing and launch

Real testing means writing dozens of hostile questions and checking the answers: the trick questions, the angry customer, the person asking about a service you discontinued, the prompt-injection attempt. Then a soft launch on one page before it goes site-wide.

The run: the bill that never stops

Monthly costs on a small-business chatbot are usually modest, but they are real and they scale with traffic.

  • Model usage. Providers bill per token — roughly, per chunk of text in and out. A short support conversation is pennies. A bot that stuffs ten pages of context into every reply costs meaningfully more per conversation, which is why retrieval quality is also a cost decision. Provider rates have been falling, not rising, which is one of the few pleasant trends here.
  • Hosting and the search index. The bot itself is a small service, and the vector or search index that powers retrieval is a managed database. Both are typically among the smaller lines on a cloud bill at small-business volumes.
  • Content refresh. When your prices, hours, services, or staff change, the index has to change. This can be automated against your website, or it can be a manual task somebody owns. Decide which before launch.
  • Monitoring and review. Someone needs to read a sample of conversations every week, at least for the first few months. This is a people cost, not a software cost, and it is where most of the value shows up — you learn what customers actually ask, in their words.
  • Escalation. Every bot needs an exit: a handoff to email, a form, or a person. That path has to be staffed or the bot becomes a dead end.

If you buy an off-the-shelf chatbot product instead, you trade most of this for a subscription — typically tiered by conversation volume. That is a legitimate choice, and we will say so when it is the right one. The trade-off is that your content, your conversation history, and your configuration live in someone else's product.

Three realistic shapes, and what changes between them

  1. The FAQ answerer. Reads your existing site, answers common questions, hands off to a contact form when stuck. Smallest build, smallest run cost, lowest risk. For most clinics, contractors, and professional services firms, this is the honest starting point.
  2. The document assistant. Answers from a real body of material — a policy manual, a product catalog, grant and bid documents, a benefits handbook. Needs genuine retrieval work and content cleanup. Often more valuable pointed inward at your staff than outward at customers.
  3. The acting agent. Looks things up and changes things in your systems. This is custom software. Price it, scope it, and test it like software — not like a website widget.

When you should not build one

We would rather tell you this up front than invoice you for it.

  • Your website cannot answer basic questions. If your hours, service area, and pricing approach are not clearly written anywhere, a chatbot has nothing to read. Fix the pages first. That is usually a custom website conversation or a website redesign, and it helps every visitor, not just the ones who open a chat window.
  • You get a handful of inquiries a day. At low volume, a human replying within the hour beats a bot, and costs less.
  • Wrong answers are expensive. Medical, legal, financial, and safety-critical advice do not belong in a general-purpose bot pointed at the public.
  • Nobody owns it. A chatbot with no internal owner goes stale in a quarter and starts quoting last year's prices.

Questions to ask before you sign anything

  • What is the one-time cost and what is the recurring cost, listed separately?
  • Who pays the model provider — you, directly, or the vendor through a markup?
  • What happens when usage doubles?
  • Do I own the prompts, the indexed content, and the code?
  • How does the bot say "I don't know," and what does it do next?
  • How do I see what it told my customers last week?
  • What does it cost to turn it off and walk away?

That last one matters. If the answer involves losing your content or your conversation history, you are renting, not buying.
