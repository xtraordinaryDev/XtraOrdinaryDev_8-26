If you run a roofing, HVAC, remodeling, or landscaping company and your website produces almost no estimate requests, the problem is rarely that nobody is looking. People in your area search for your trade every day. The problem is what happens in the ten seconds after they land on your site. Most contractor websites lose that moment, and they lose it for a handful of predictable reasons. Here they are, in the order we would fix them.

One note before the list. Everything below applies whether your site is a page builder template, something a nephew made in 2016, or a Facebook page standing in for a website. The fixes are the same. The question is only whether the current site can be patched or needs to be rebuilt, and we cover that at the end.

1. The phone number is not the first thing a visitor can tap

Open your site on your own phone. Count how many taps it takes to call you. If the answer is more than one, you are losing the people who are ready right now, and those are the best leads you will ever get.

A homeowner with water coming through the ceiling is not going to read your About page. They want a number, and they want it in the top corner of the screen and in a bar at the bottom that stays put while they scroll. Every screen. Not just the home page.

Fix first: a tap-to-call button in the header and a sticky bar on mobile with two buttons: call and request an estimate. This single change tends to do more than any redesign.

2. The estimate form goes to an inbox nobody watches

Plenty of contractor sites do have a form. The trouble is where it goes. If it lands in an email account you check at the end of the day, the homeowner has already called the next company on the list. Speed to respond is the whole game in home services.

The form should text you, or whoever books jobs, the moment it is submitted. It should also ask only for what you need to call them back: name, phone, what they need done, and the town. Every extra field costs you submissions. Save the measurements and photos for the call.

3. Nothing on the page proves you are legitimate

Homeowners are nervous about contractors. They have heard the stories. In the first few seconds they are scanning for signals that you are real, local, licensed, and still in business. If those signals are missing, they leave even if your work is excellent.

The signals that matter, roughly in order:

  • Real photos of real jobs. Your crew, your trucks, your finished work. Stock photos of a smiling man in a hard hat are a signal too, and it is the wrong one.
  • Reviews, visible without clicking. Pull your Google reviews onto the page. A rating and a few recent quotes near the top do more than a testimonials page three clicks deep.
  • License number, insurance, and the areas you serve. Boring, and essential. Put them where they can be seen.
  • A face and a name. People hire people. A photo of the owner with two sentences about the company outperforms a paragraph of adjectives.

4. The site is slow on a phone

Most of your visitors are on a phone, often on a job site or in a parking lot with a weak signal. A site that takes several seconds to show anything gets closed before it loads. Page builder themes are the usual culprit: they ship a lot of code the page never uses, plus full-size photos that were never resized.

You can test this yourself. Turn off Wi-Fi, open your site on mobile data, and count. If you are waiting, so is everyone else. This is also one of the factors Google weighs when deciding which local sites to show, so it affects how many people arrive in the first place.

5. One page tries to cover every service

Someone searching for "basement finishing" and someone searching for "kitchen remodel" are two different customers with two different questions. If both land on a general home page that lists eight services in a row, neither feels like they found the right place.

A page per service, with its own photos, its own common questions, and its own estimate button, does two things. It converts better because it speaks to one need. And it gives Google a clear page to show for that search, instead of a vague home page competing against everyone.

The same logic applies to location. If you serve the whole metro, a short page for each major area you work in, with real jobs from that area, helps you show up for people who search with a city name attached.

6. The headline is about you, not about them

"Quality craftsmanship since 2009" is a headline about the company. "Roof repair in the East Metro, usually within a week, with a written estimate before any work starts" is a headline about the homeowner's problem. The second one gets the call.

Write the headline you would say out loud if a neighbor asked what you do and where. Then put the strongest reason to trust you directly under it. That is the entire top of the page. Everything else can come after.

7. Your Google Business Profile and your website disagree

A surprising number of estimate requests start on the map listing, not the website. If your Business Profile has an old phone number, a different business name, inconsistent hours, or a link to a dead page, you lose people before they ever see your site. Match the name, number, address, and hours exactly, link the profile to the specific page that fits, and keep adding job photos there as well. The profile and the site should feel like the same company.

What to fix first, in order

  1. Tap-to-call in the header and a sticky mobile bar. Hours of work, immediate effect.
  2. Route the estimate form to a text message. Same day.
  3. Put reviews, license, and service area near the top of the home page.
  4. Replace stock images with your own job photos, resized so they load fast.
  5. Rewrite the headline around the customer's problem and your area.
  6. Add one page per core service, then pages for your main service areas.
  7. Clean up the Google Business Profile so it matches the site.

If you do only the first two, you will likely notice a difference in the number of calls. The rest compound over the following months as Google picks up the new pages.

Patch it or rebuild it?

Some sites can take these fixes. If your current site loads quickly, you can edit it yourself, and the structure is sound, patch it. Add the call bar, fix the form, swap the photos, and move on with your week.

Rebuild it when any of these are true: the site is slow and nobody knows why, you cannot make simple edits without calling someone, it was built on a page builder that fights every change, or it was never designed for a phone in the first place. In those cases the patches cost more than they return, and a clean rebuild that keeps your existing Google rankings intact is the better path.
