The honest answer is that a first release takes as long as the smallest useful version of your idea takes to build, test, and put in front of real users. A single-purpose internal tool that replaces one spreadsheet is a short project. A customer portal with logins, billing, documents, notifications, and an admin back end is a much longer one. The technology almost never sets the schedule. Scope, decision speed, and the state of your data do.

Below is what actually happens between a kickoff call and a working first release, what stretches a schedule, and how to plan a project so the date means something.

Why nobody can give you a number in the first phone call

When someone asks us how long a project takes, what they usually mean is "when can my team stop doing this by hand?" That is a fair question. But a request like "we need a portal so clients can see their job status" can describe a two-screen tool or a year-long platform, depending on answers we do not have yet.

The questions that move the date the most are rarely technical:

  • How many types of user are there? One internal role is simple. Admin, staff, contractor, and customer means four sets of permissions, four sets of screens, and four sets of edge cases.
  • What does it have to talk to? A standalone app is straightforward. An app that syncs with your accounting system, your scheduling software, and a payment processor inherits the quirks of all three.
  • Where does the data live now? Clean data in one system is fast to bring over. Twelve years of spreadsheets with inconsistent naming is its own project.
  • Who signs off? One decision-maker keeps a project moving. A committee that meets monthly sets the pace no matter how fast the code gets written.

This is why we write a fixed scope before we write code. The scope document is where those questions get answered, and the schedule falls out of the answers rather than out of a guess.

The phases of a first release

1. Discovery and written scope

We sit down with the people who will actually use the thing, not just the person paying for it. We watch the current process, including the workarounds nobody mentions in meetings. Then we write down what version one does, what it explicitly does not do, and what "done" looks like for each piece.

This is the shortest phase in calendar time and the one that saves the most of it. A feature cut here costs nothing. The same feature cut after it is half built costs real money.

2. Data model and screen flow

Before anything looks like software, we decide how the information is structured: what a job is, what a customer is, how an invoice relates to both. Getting this wrong is the single most expensive mistake in a software project, because everything built on top of it has to be reworked.

Alongside that, we map the screens. Not polished visuals yet, just the order of operations, so you can see whether the flow matches how your business actually runs.

3. Build in slices

We build in vertical slices rather than layers. Instead of finishing all the database work, then all the back end, then all the screens, we take one complete workflow, such as "create a job and assign it to a crew," and make it work end to end. Then the next one.

The point is that you get something you can click on early and often. You do not wait until the end to discover that the approval step does not match how approvals work in your office.

4. Testing, migration, and training

Teams routinely underestimate this phase. It includes moving your existing records in, checking them, fixing the ones that will not import cleanly, and then getting your staff comfortable enough that they do not quietly keep using the old spreadsheet.

If you are bringing over years of history, plan for migration to be a named line item with its own time, not something that happens on launch weekend.

5. Launch and the first weeks after

Real use surfaces things no test plan catches. Someone pastes a phone number with dashes. A field crew loses signal mid-form. A customer uploads a 90 MB photo. The weeks right after launch are for fixing those, not for starting phase two.

What stretches a schedule

  • Scope that grows quietly. Not one big change, but twenty small "while you're in there" requests. Each is reasonable. Together they are a second project.
  • Waiting on third parties. API credentials, merchant accounts, IT approvals, and vendor support tickets are often the longest single delay in a project, and they are outside everyone's control.
  • Content and data you owe us. Logos, legal copy, price lists, and the clean export of your old records. Work stalls politely while these sit in someone's inbox.
  • Slow feedback loops. If review takes two weeks each round, the calendar doubles regardless of how fast development goes.
  • Compliance requirements discovered late. Health data, payment data, and anything involving minors bring rules that change the architecture. Say so on day one.

What speeds it up

  • One decision-maker with authority to say yes, plus one person inside your team who owns getting us what we need.
  • A ruthless version one. Ask of every feature: if this were missing on launch day, would we still use the system? If yes, it is phase two.
  • Reusing what works. Payments, email, authentication, and document storage do not need to be invented. Mature services handle them, and we wire them in.
  • Accepting a manual step for now. An export button that someone clicks once a week is a fraction of the work of a live two-way integration, and it can carry you for a year.

A useful way to size your project

Rather than asking for a number up front, describe your project by shape. A tool that replaces one spreadsheet for one team is the smallest shape. Add outside users and it gets bigger. Add money moving through it and it gets bigger again. Add integrations with systems you do not control and it gets bigger still. Each of those steps changes the schedule more than any choice of framework.

Industry-wide, teams commonly deliver a narrow first release and then extend it over time. That pattern exists because it works: you learn more from three months of real use than from three months of extra planning. You can see the range of shapes in our previous work, from event and contractor sites to full procurement and AI research platforms.

Red flags in a timeline estimate

  • A date with no written scope behind it. A date without a definition of done is a wish.
  • No mention of testing, migration, or training. These are not optional, so if they are not in the plan, they are hiding in the overrun.
  • An estimate that does not change when you add features. That means nobody is actually costing the work.
  • No named dependencies. A good plan lists what it needs from you and by when.
